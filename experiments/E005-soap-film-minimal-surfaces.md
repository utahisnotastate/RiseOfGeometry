# E005 · Soap Films: Nature Solves Geometry Problems Instantly

**Shows:** Physical systems find minimum-energy shapes, and those shapes are geometric solutions. ([Concepts 04](../docs/concepts/04-shape-picks-the-notes.md), [13](../docs/concepts/13-can-geometry-replace-physics.md))
**Status:** ✅ Established (Plateau's laws, 1873; proved by Jean Taylor, 1976).

## You'll need
- Pipe cleaners or thin wire
- Bubble mix (water + dish soap + a spoon of glycerin or sugar)
- Two clear plastic sheets and some short pegs (for the Steiner-tree demo)

## Demos
1. **Cube frame:** Build a wire cube and dip it. The film forms a small square in the middle connected to every edge. It never simply fills the six faces.
2. **Tetrahedron frame:** Dip it. Six films meet at the center.
3. **Plateau's laws:** Everywhere films meet, **exactly three** meet along a line at **120°**, and **four** lines meet at a point at about **109.47°** (the tetrahedral angle, the same angle as the bonds in a methane molecule).
4. **Shortest road network (Steiner tree):** Put pegs between two plastic sheets at the corners of a square. Dip and lift. The film forms the **shortest network connecting all four pegs**, with two junction points at 120°, not the X you might guess. This is a hard problem for a computer. Soap solves it in a blink (though not always the *global* best; try it several times).

## Why it matters
Surface tension makes the film minimize its area. So the soap is "computing" a solution to a geometry problem. Architect Frei Otto used soap films to design the Munich Olympic Stadium roof (1972).

## Going further
Measure the angles in photos with a protractor app. How close to 120° do you get?
