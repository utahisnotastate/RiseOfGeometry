# E003 · Vortex Ring Cannon

**Shows:** Toroidal vortices are self-stabilizing and carry momentum a long way. ([Concepts 07](../docs/concepts/07-topology-knots-and-whole-numbers.md), [08](../docs/concepts/08-vortices-and-tori.md))
**Status of the effect:** ✅ Established (Helmholtz 1858, Kelvin 1867).

## You'll need
- A cardboard box (a shoebox works; bigger is better)
- Plastic wrap or a shower cap, and tape
- A craft knife
- Optional: fog from a fog machine, incense smoke (adult supervision), or water vapor from a cool-mist humidifier to make the rings visible
- A candle or a row of lightweight paper strips as targets

## Method
1. Cut a circular hole about 1/4 to 1/3 the width of the box in one face.
2. Replace the opposite face with plastic wrap stretched tight and taped.
3. Fill with fog (optional). Tap the plastic sharply.
4. Aim at the candle or paper strips 2–5 m away.

## What to measure
- **Range vs. hole size.** Try holes of 3 cm, 5 cm, and 8 cm. Which travels farthest?
- **Speed.** Film at high frame rate (most phones do 240 fps) with a meter stick in view.
- **Collisions.** Fire two rings at each other, or one at a wall, and watch them spread and slow.

## Why it works
The air spins around a circular core. There are no "ends" for the spin to leak out of, so the ring keeps its shape. Kelvin's circulation theorem says an ideal ring would last forever. Real air has viscosity, so it slowly decays.

## Safety
Use candles only with adult supervision and away from anything flammable.
